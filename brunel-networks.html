Why I am writing this: these notes follow the progression of Dr. Jyotika Bahuguna's Introduction to Neurocomputational Modeling (I) lecture: start with a simple neuron, make explicit modelling choices, connect neurons, and ask what collective behaviour emerges.

01 · Why model a neuron?

A model is a way of turning an intuition into something that can be tested. The lecture frames modelling as a tool for understanding mechanisms, making qualitative and quantitative predictions, exploring “what if?” worlds, and building theory from simpler rules.

“There is no best model. There is a best model for a question.”

— framing used in Dr. Bahuguna's lecture around reduced neuronal models.

02 · The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron

The first abstraction is to treat the membrane potential as a dynamical variable. The neuron integrates input, leaks toward its resting state, and emits a spike when a threshold is reached.

τm dv/dt = −(v − Vrest) + Iext/g

τm

The membrane timescale: how quickly the membrane responds.

Iext

External drive. More drive generally makes threshold crossings easier to reach.

Threshold + reset

When the model reaches threshold, it emits a spike and is reset.

03 · From one neuron to a network

Synapses turn each spike into a change in another neuron's future trajectory. The lecture emphasizes both synaptic strength and synaptic timescales as modelling choices.

dse/dt = −se/τsyn     dsi/dt = −si/τsyn

For the network used in the summer project, excitatory events add a synaptic contribution +J, while inhibitory events contribute −gJ. The parameter g therefore controls the relative strength of inhibition.

04 · The Brunel network

The model is a mesoscale recurrent network containing excitatory and inhibitory populations. In the implementation used for the project:

2,000 E + 500 I

A total population of 2,500 neurons.

Random connectivity

Connection probability ε = 0.1 for the recurrent E/I connections.

Poisson drive

Stochastic background input provides ongoing external drive to the population.

05 · What does “network state” mean?

One useful distinction is between synchrony and regularity. Synchrony asks whether neurons spike together. Regularity asks whether a neuron's spikes occur at roughly regular intervals.

06 · Where can oscillations come from?

The interesting question is not “how do I add a 20 Hz oscillator?” but “can recurrent E/I interactions create a preferred timescale on their own?” The lecture shows that changing the inhibitory-to-excitatory balance can move a network between different activity regimes, including asynchronous and synchronous regimes.

The key intuition: excitation pushes activity forward; inhibition pushes it back. Their feedback, delays and finite synaptic timescales can favour particular temporal patterns.

07 · The modelling choices I played with

g

Relative inhibitory strength. In the project sweep, g = 4.5 gave the strongest response around the explored ~20 Hz region.

τsyn

Synaptic decay time. Increasing it kept the dominant frequency relatively stable in the explored range while flattening the spectral peak.

w

Adaptation strength. Increasing adaptation reduced response amplitude and shifted the preferred resonant frequency lower in the project simulations.

08 · Spikes → population rate → spectrum

The notebook records spike times and population firing rate, removes the transient and mean, then computes a Fourier amplitude spectrum from the population activity. That turns a visually messy raster into a compact question: which temporal scales dominate the population?

09 · What I take away

This model is deliberately reduced. Its value is that it gives me a controlled virtual laboratory: change a parameter, rerun the network, and see which aspects of collective behaviour move with it.

My current interpretation: the model suggests that collective resonance is a property of the interaction between intrinsic dynamics, synaptic timescales and E/I balance—not a property of a single neuron in isolation.
